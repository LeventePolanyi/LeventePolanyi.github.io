Why I built it

I spent two years producing interest rate risk reporting at a bank. The numbers came out of a system, and I was comfortable explaining what they meant without ever having built the calculation behind them. That bothered me more the longer it went on.

So I built it from the Basel standard, to find out whether I actually understood what I had been reporting. The answer was mostly yes. The part I had underestimated is how much of the result comes from behavioural assumptions rather than from the arithmetic.

What this measures

ΔEVE — economic value of equity

The present value of every repricing cashflow on the balance sheet, revalued under a shocked curve. It is a point-in-time valuation measure: it asks what the bank's equity is worth today if rates move and nothing else changes. Long-dated positions dominate, because discounting bites hardest at the far end.

ΔNII — net interest income

The earnings effect over the next twelve months, driven only by positions that actually reprice inside that window. A bank can look fine on ΔEVE and still take an earnings hit, or the reverse. Basel assesses NII under the two parallel shocks only.

A bank whose worst-case ΔEVE exceeds 15% of Tier 1 capital is flagged as a supervisory outlier. That is not a breach of a hard limit; it is the trigger for supervisory attention and, usually, a conversation about hedging the duration gap.

The two measures point in opposite directions, and that is the interesting part. A parallel rise costs HSBC $9.4bn of economic value but adds $4.4bn of banking net interest income. A parallel fall does the reverse. That is the signature of a deposit-funded bank: rate rises improve the margin earned on an enormous non-maturity deposit base, while depressing the present value of the long-dated assets those deposits fund.

Their disclosure puts numbers on the assumptions that drive it. Deposits are modelled with a 50% pass-on rate, so only half of any rate move reaches the customer. Non-maturity deposits carry an average repricing maturity of 17 months and a longest of 120 months, against a contractual maturity of zero. The NII figures assume a static balance sheet with no migration from current accounts into term deposits. Each of those is a judgement, and each moves the answer more than the shock size does.

The worst case, $9,449m under parallel up, is 6.2% of Tier 1 capital, well inside the 15% outlier threshold.

Method, and what it leaves out

Ten bands rather than nineteen
BCBS 368 specifies nineteen time bands. This uses ten, with the same midpoint convention. The arithmetic is identical; the resolution is coarser.
No behavioural modelling
Non-maturity deposits are slotted at their contractual band. In practice this is the single largest modelling judgement in any IRRBB framework: how much of a current account balance is genuinely overnight money and how much is stable for years. Basel caps the share that can be treated as core, and the assumption usually moves the answer more than the shock does.
No optionality
Prepayment on fixed-rate loans and early withdrawal on term deposits are both ignored. Both are rate-dependent, and both work against the bank.
No basis risk
Every position is discounted off one curve. A real book has assets priced off one index and liabilities off another, and the spread between them moves.
Single currency
Aggregation across currencies has its own rules. This values one book.
